Somewhere between the death of Google Reader and the rise of TikTok, we collectively forgot that the internet used to be a place you explored. Not scrolled. Explored.

But the small web never actually died. It just got quiet.

## What is the small web?

It's personal blogs. It's hand-coded HTML pages with visitor counters. It's webrings, digital gardens, and wikis maintained by one person for years. It's everything that doesn't have a signup form, a cookie banner the size of your screen, or a "subscribe to our newsletter" popup.

## Where to start

Marginalia Search is a search engine that deliberately filters out commercial content. Search for anything and you'll find pages written by humans who care about the topic, not SEO consultants who care about rankings.

The Yesterweb and the IndieWeb community maintain directories of personal sites. Neocities — a spiritual successor to Geocities — hosts thousands of handmade websites, many of them genuinely creative.

## Why it matters

The big web optimizes for engagement. The small web optimizes for expression. When someone builds a page about their favorite trains, or documents every meal they cooked in 2025, they're not trying to convert you. They're sharing something because they want to.

There's a different quality of attention when you read something like that. It's slower, less anxious, more human.

## Build your own corner

You don't need a framework. You don't need a CMS. An HTML file, a CSS file, and a cheap hosting plan is enough. If you want something slightly more structured, a static site generator like Hugo or Rocket will do the job.

The bar for entry is exactly where it should be: low enough that anyone can do it, high enough that only people who care will bother.
